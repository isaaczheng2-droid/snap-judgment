Minimum confidence
Read this before you put money on anything. “Confident” is not “safe,” and it is not “profitable.” Two facts from the Accuracy tab apply here. First, against the spread this model is a coin flip — 52.5% over 1,812 graded games, against a 52.4% break-even, with a confidence interval that straddles it — which is why there are no spread picks on this page at all. Second, straight-up confidence is well calibrated, but a favorite that wins 79% of the time is priced by the market at roughly −376 on the moneyline; to make money you would have to find a book paying better than that, and books do not usually. What this page tells you is which games the model is sure about. It does not tell you which bets have value. Not betting advice.

The honest verdict

It is a real model, and it is worse than Vegas. Picking winners at 61.8% is decisively better than chance (p < 10−20) and nine points better than blindly taking the home team. But the closing market line picks at 66.6%, and a McNemar test on the paired predictions puts that gap far outside noise (p < 0.0001). When the two disagree, the market is right three times in five.

Against the spread there is still nothing here worth acting on. 52.5% — 951–861 — which sits one tenth of a point above the 52.4% you need merely to break even on standard juice, with a 95% confidence interval of 50.2–54.8% that comfortably contains break-even. Season by season it runs from 46.3% to 57.0%, which is what noise looks like. Adding the scheme features moved this figure up from 50.4%, and that is the most flattering number in this audit, which is exactly why it deserves the most suspicion: one feature set, tested once, on one seven-year sample, landing a rounding error past the line that would make it free money. Filtering to the games where the model disagrees most with the line does not rescue it either — at gaps of five points or more it goes 52.0% over 510 games. Treat it as a coin flip.

It is overconfident in the middle of its range. When it says a team has a 65–75% chance, that team wins meaningfully less often than that. Only its strongest calls hold up: above 70% confidence it roughly matches the market on those games.

Where it does hold up is the long view. Summed across a season its win probabilities reconstruct team records with a correlation of 0.80 and an average miss of 1.73 wins. Its largest misses are the teams that famously outran their underlying numbers — the 2022 Vikings (predicted 8.5 wins, actual 13) and the 2024 Vikings (9.5 vs. 14), both of whom went absurdly well in one-score games. Missing those in that direction is what a working efficiency model is supposed to do.

Method

Team ratings

EPA per play — offense and defense-allowed, split by pass and rush — from nflverse team-week box scores, smoothed with a six-game exponential average. At each season’s start a team is 50% its final rating from last year and 50% the league mean, which is why Week 1 ratings are flatter than 2025 form.

Game model

Gradient-boosted trees on rating differentials, rest-day advantage, the division-game flag, the projected starting quarterback’s own passing and rushing form, whether a team is starting someone other than last week’s quarterback, the share of each side’s recent snaps ruled out on the injury report, and six scheme-identity measures describing how each staff plays — pass-run balance, air yards per attempt, tempo, pass rush, protection and takeaways, each an exponentially-weighted average of that team’s prior games only. Weather is collected and shown but deliberately kept out of the model — it tested as noise. The predicted score splits the market total by the predicted margin.

Player model

Each player’s rolling stat line, adjusted by the opponent’s defensive EPA allowed at the relevant skill, via ridge regression — three terms, which is why every projection can be shown broken into a baseline, his form, the matchup and the venue that add back to the number. Week 1 lines use 2025 season-end form matched to each player’s current team and depth-chart slot.

What each added factor was worth

Walk-forward, 2019–2025, same protocol throughout. Each row is the feature set as a whole, scored on identical folds, so the rows are comparable to each other and to nothing else.

Quarterback identity moved accuracy 61.0% → 61.8%, but McNemar on the paired picks returned p = 0.30 — directionally right, statistically unproven. Injuries sharpened the probabilities without improving the picks. Weather is the clean negative result: it changed the pick rate by 0.00% and made Brier worse, so it is shown on the site as context and never fed to the model — both teams play in the same wind, so it mostly cancels. Coaching scheme is the newest addition and the most honest summary is that it bought calibration, not picks: Brier 0.2316 → 0.2303 and margin error 10.54 → 10.48, while only 275 of 1,855 games changed hands, 143–132 in its favour — p = 0.55, indistinguishable from a coin toss. It is in the model because better-calibrated probabilities are worth having on their own. It is not in because it makes the picks better, and it does not.
