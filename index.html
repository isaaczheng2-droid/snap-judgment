The honest verdict, in full

The honest verdict

It is a real model, and it is worse than Vegas. Picking winners at 64.4% is decisively better than chance (p < 10−20) and nine points better than blindly taking the home team. But the closing market line picks at 66.6%, and a McNemar test on the paired predictions puts that gap far outside noise (p < 0.0001). When the two disagree, the market is right three times in five.

Against the spread there is nothing here worth acting on, and this page can now show you why in one line. 53.2% — 965–847 — against the 52.4% you need merely to break even on standard juice, with a 95% confidence interval of 50.9–55.6% that contains break-even. Season by season it runs from 47.6% to 57.8%.

Watch what this number did in a single day of work. It read 52.7%, just above break-even. Adding a track-record rating — which improved picks, calibration and margin error together — sent it down to 51.3%, below break-even. Adjusting the ratings for opponent, which improved all three again, sent it back up to 53.2%. Three model versions, all strictly better than the last on every measure this page trusts, and the spread figure went down, then up, across break-even both times.

That is what a number with no signal in it looks like when you keep poking the model. It is also the most flattering figure in this audit, and successive feature additions walked it up from 50.4%, each kept partly because numbers like this one moved — so it is selected on, and biased upward by construction. Filtering to the games where the model disagrees most with the line flatters it further, 55.6% at gaps of five points or more over 421 games, which is how every spurious betting system ever found has looked at this stage. Treat all of it as a coin flip, and treat anyone quoting a number like this one without its history as someone who has not looked.

It is overconfident, and the obvious fix does not work. When the model alone says 75%+ it wins 75.4% of the time — but it says 83.6%, an eight-point overstatement across 358 games, and at 65–75% it overstates by another six. The textbook correction is isotonic regression: learn the curve from past seasons and bend the number back. Fitted strictly walk-forward, it made things worse — Brier 0.2262 → 0.2292, log loss 0.6468 → 0.6599. The miscalibration is not stable enough from season to season for a curve fitted on old ones to transfer, so it is not applied here.

What does fix it is the blend. Mixing with the closing line cuts the calibration error roughly in half on its own, which is most of why the published number is trustworthier than the model behind it.

The blend weight was a guess, and it was too generous to the model. It sat at 40% model, 60% market because nobody had checked. Sweeping it over the whole audit, the pick rate peaks at 20% and Brier and log loss both improve there too, so the site now uses 20. Fitting the weight walk-forward, where it never sees the season it is scoring, chose 15% in each of the last three seasons — the same region. The uncomfortable part is worth stating plainly: log loss on its own is minimised at about 5%. The model buys roughly half a point of pick rate over the bare market and buys nothing at all in probability quality.

Where it does hold up is the long view. Summed across a season its win probabilities reconstruct team records with a correlation of 0.80 and an average miss of 1.61 wins. Its largest misses are the teams that famously outran their underlying numbers — the 2024 Vikings (predicted 8.9 wins, actual 14) and the 2025 Patriots (9.1 vs. 14), both of whom went absurdly well in one-score games. Missing those in that direction is what a working efficiency model is supposed to do.

What each added factor was worth

Walk-forward, 2019–2025, same protocol throughout. Each row is the feature set as a whole, scored on identical folds, so the rows are comparable to each other and to nothing else.

Quarterback identity moved accuracy 61.0% → 61.8%, but McNemar on the paired picks returned p = 0.30 — directionally right, statistically unproven. Weather is the clean negative result: it changed the pick rate by 0.00% and made Brier worse, so it is shown on the site as context and never fed to the model — both teams play in the same wind, so it mostly cancels. Injuries were actively harmful for most of this project’s life. The first version summed every ruled-out player’s snap share, which made five rotational backups look like one lost left tackle and treated a starting corner like a starting guard; it picked winners worse than having no injury features at all (61.2% against 61.8%). Weighting the two biggest absences per side by position fixed the sign of that mistake, and this table stays honest that the injury row is the shakiest in it. Coaching scheme, offensive and then defensive, bought calibration and not picks: Brier 0.2341 → 0.2315 → 0.2302 and margin error 10.59 → 10.44 → 10.41, while the pick rate lands at 61.3%, slightly below where the offensive block alone had it. Both are in because better-calibrated probabilities are worth having on their own. Neither is in because it improves the picks, because neither does. The defensive block was also tested as a player-prop input, including as a predictor of carries and targets, where the weighted error moved by 0.001 — how well a defense plays is already in that model as EPA allowed, and it contains nearly everything that how it plays would add.

The last row is the humbling one. Every factor above describes how a team plays. None of them had ever been checked against the dumbest available alternative: a rating that knows only who beat whom, updated after each game, with no EPA, no quarterback, no injuries and no scheme. That rating picks 63.3% on its own — a hair better than the entire feature set it took this whole project to build, with a better Brier and a better log loss.

Reading that as “ratings beat features” would be wrong, and the check that shows why is worth more than the result. Freeze the same rating at the start of each season, so it knows only prior years, and it drops to 60.2% — worse than the feature model. All of its advantage comes from updating after every game. What it is really measuring is that a rating system tracks in-season form better than exponentially-weighted EPA does, using exactly what EPA per play throws away: the result, including special teams, field position and every close game that went one way rather than the other.

The two disagree on 24% of games and the rating takes those 53–47, so they know different things, and combining them was worth more than any factor added before it: picks 61.3% → 63.3%, Brier 0.2302 → 0.2269, margin error 10.41 → 10.30. Two things stop that being a victory lap. On the blended picks McNemar returns p = 0.14 — better in five of seven seasons but unproven. And the rating alone, once blended with the market, adds nothing at all (66.6%, the market’s own number): it is largely telling the line what the line already knows. The features are what make the combination beat it.

The last row came from taking that lesson seriously. If a rating system won partly because it adjusts for opponent automatically, then the EPA ratings not doing so at all was a real hole: they were a plain average of a team’s own per-game efficiency, so a team that had played three of the worst defenses in the league simply looked good. Solving offense and defense together from every game — each team’s efficiency against each opponent’s, using only games already played — and swapping those in for the raw versions took picks to 64.4%, Brier to 0.2259, margin error to 10.24, and the blended number to 67.1% against the market’s 66.6%. It cost nothing in complexity: the same five features, better estimates of the same five things.

Play-by-play detail was the obvious next thing to try, and it did nothing. Everything above is built from nflverse’s weekly team totals, which carry EPA but not the shape of how it was earned. Five measures that only exist at the play level were built and tested: success rate, explosive-play rate, early-down EPA, third-down conversion and red-zone efficiency, each for a team’s own offense and for what its defense allowed, smoothed exactly like the EPA ratings. The reasoning was sound — EPA per play is a mean, and a mean cannot tell a team that is steadily fine from one that is mostly bad with three explosions.

All five together made it worse: Brier 0.2259 → 0.2266, log loss 0.6453 → 0.6472, picks flat. Split into smaller blocks nothing survived either. Success and explosive rate moved picks +0.2 points, inside the ±0.4 of seed noise, with Brier and log loss unchanged to four decimals. The situational pair looked best on picks at +0.5 points but made the probabilities worse on both measures (p = 0.37), which is the signature of a feature that is fitting the sample rather than the game. The code is kept but not wired in, because downloading 220 MB of play-by-play every run to move nothing is not free.

One caveat applies to every row above. A single XGBoost fit varies by roughly ±0.4 points of pick accuracy from the random seed alone — wider than most of the effects in this table. Every number here is now the mean of an eight-seed ensemble, which is also what the site runs. Before that change the defensive block was measured on one fit, looked clearly useless, and was published here as such; the ensemble reversed it. Any result on this page resting on a difference smaller than half a point should be read as unproven.

Player prop value, and what it is actually worth

The projections are compared against FanDuel's posted lines. This measures whether that comparison has ever been worth anything.

There are no historical FanDuel lines to test against — books do not publish archives. So the test sets a line at each player's own rolling median and asks whether the model picks the right side. Now the part that matters more. Betting the under blindly on every one of those same lines returned —, already past break-even. A line that a one-word strategy beats is a soft line, and no sportsbook posts one. Every number in the paragraph above was measured against that soft line. None of it demonstrates an edge over FanDuel, and nothing on this site has ever been tested against a real sportsbook's prop pricing.

What the test does establish is narrower and still worth having: the projections carry real information about which side of a naive number a player lands on, the effect is present on overs and unders separately rather than being an artifact of these stats being right-skewed, and the probability attached to each prop is calibrated — when it says 70% it wins close to 70%, with an expected calibration error of —. Two consequences worth knowing before using any of it: roughly — of qualifying props are unders, and touchdown props are never recommended at all, because those two models lose to a rolling average of the player's own recent games.

About Snap Judgment

How the predictions are made, what the words on this site mean, and what it does not claim.

How predictions work Methodology Definitions

Every game gets one number first: the probability that each team wins. That number is a blend of two things. Eighty percent of it is the closing market line, which is the most accurate public forecast of an NFL game that exists. Twenty percent is our own model, which reads each team’s opponent-adjusted efficiency, its results-based track record, its starting quarterback, its injury report and its coaching tendencies. The blend picks winners slightly more often than the market does (67.1% against 66.6% over 1,855 graded games) and is better calibrated than the model on its own. The Model Performance page shows all of this, including the parts that do not flatter us.

Player projections are made from football data alone. A player’s recent form, how much he is used, the opponent’s defense at that skill and the venue combine into one projected number, and every projection can be taken apart into exactly those pieces. Around each number is an expected range, drawn from how far past projections of that stat have actually missed.

Only then do we look at the sportsbook. The Props page compares finished projections against FanDuel’s lines. The projection is never moved toward the line. A prop is only flagged when the odds are inside a sensible range, the model’s probability clears 65%, the projection is meaningfully off the line, and the combined bet value score clears 65. Most weeks most props do not qualify, and the page says so rather than forcing a pick.

Two words are kept apart on every screen. Model confidence is how sure the model is about the underlying prediction. Bet value is how attractive the posted line looks relative to that prediction. A projection can have high confidence and no betting value at all, because the sportsbook already agrees with it.

What this site does not claim. No bet is guaranteed, safe, certain or risk-free, and nothing here is betting advice. Every historical figure is published with its sample size and, where it matters, its confidence interval. The prop backtest has only been run against a proxy line, not against FanDuel itself, and the Props page says so on every card.

Team ratings

EPA per play — offense and defense-allowed, split by pass and rush — from nflverse team-week box scores, smoothed with a six-game exponential average. At each season’s start a team is 50% its final rating from last year and 50% the league mean, which is why Week 1 ratings are flatter than 2025 form.

Game model

Gradient-boosted trees on rating differentials, rest-day advantage, the division-game flag, the projected starting quarterback’s own passing and rushing form, whether a team is starting someone other than last week’s quarterback, the share of each side’s recent snaps ruled out on the injury report, and six scheme-identity measures describing how each staff plays — pass-run balance, air yards per attempt, tempo, pass rush, protection and takeaways, each an exponentially-weighted average of that team’s prior games only. Weather is collected and shown but deliberately kept out of the model — it tested as noise. The predicted score splits the market total by the predicted margin.

Player model

Each player’s rolling stat line, adjusted by the opponent’s defensive EPA allowed at the relevant skill, via ridge regression — three terms, which is why every projection can be shown broken into a baseline, his form, the matchup and the venue that add back to the number. Week 1 lines use 2025 season-end form matched to each player’s current team and depth-chart slot.
