The honest verdict

It is a real model, and it is worse than Vegas. Picking winners at 61.2% is decisively better than chance (p < 10−20) and eight points better than blindly taking the home team. But the closing market line picks at 66.6%, and a McNemar test on the paired predictions puts that gap far outside noise (p < 0.0001). When the two disagree, the market is right about three times in five.

Against the spread it has no edge at all. 50.4%, with a 95% confidence interval of 48.1–52.8% — statistically indistinguishable from flipping a coin, and below the 52.4% needed to break even on standard juice. Filtering to only the games where the model disagrees most with the line does not help: at disagreements of five points or more it goes exactly 50.0%. This is the result that matters most, and it is the one most prediction sites do not publish.

It is overconfident in the middle of its range. When it says a team has a 65–75% chance, that team wins meaningfully less often than that. Only its strongest calls hold up: above 70% confidence it roughly matches the market on those games.

Where it does hold up is the long view. Summed across a season its win probabilities reconstruct team records with a correlation of 0.81 and an average miss of 1.75 wins. Its largest misses are the teams that famously outran their underlying numbers — the 2024 Chiefs (predicted 9.9 wins, actual 15) and the 2022 Vikings (8.5 vs. 13), both of whom went absurdly well in one-score games. Missing those in that direction is what a working efficiency model is supposed to do.

Method

Team ratings

EPA per play — offense and defense-allowed, split by pass and rush — from nflverse team-week box scores, smoothed with a six-game exponential average. At each season’s start a team is 50% its final rating from last year and 50% the league mean, which is why Week 1 ratings are flatter than 2025 form.

Game model

Gradient-boosted trees on rating differentials, rest-day advantage, the division-game flag, the projected starting quarterback’s own passing and rushing form, whether a team is starting someone other than last week’s quarterback, and the share of each side’s recent snaps ruled out on the injury report. Weather is collected and shown but deliberately kept out of the model — it tested as noise. The predicted score splits the market total by the predicted margin.

Player model

Each player’s rolling stat line, adjusted by the opponent’s defensive EPA allowed at the relevant skill, via ridge regression. Week 1 lines use 2025 season-end form matched to each player’s current team and depth-chart slot.

What each added factor was worth

Walk-forward, 2019–2025, same protocol throughout. Quarterback identity is the only addition that improved every measure at once — though not by enough to call it proven.

Adding the starting QB’s own form moved accuracy 61.0% → 61.8% and against-the-spread 50.4% → 52.2%, but a McNemar test on the paired picks returns p = 0.30: only 179 of 1,855 games changed hands, 97–82 in the new model’s favour. Directionally right on every metric, statistically unproven on the one that counts. Weather changed nothing, exactly as expected — both teams play in the same wind. Injuries sharpened the probabilities slightly (best Brier of any set) without improving the picks.
