Minimum confidence
Read this before you put money on anything. “Confident” is not “safe,” and it is not “profitable.” Two facts from the Accuracy tab apply here. First, against the spread this model is a coin flip — 52.7% over 1,812 graded games, against a 52.4% break-even, with a confidence interval that straddles it — which is why there are no spread picks on this page at all. Second, straight-up confidence is well calibrated, but a favorite that wins 79% of the time is priced by the market at roughly −376 on the moneyline; to make money you would have to find a book paying better than that, and books do not usually. What this page tells you is which games the model is sure about. It does not tell you which bets have value. Not betting advice.

The honest verdict

It is a real model, and it is worse than Vegas. Picking winners at 61.9% is decisively better than chance (p < 10−20) and nine points better than blindly taking the home team. But the closing market line picks at 66.6%, and a McNemar test on the paired predictions puts that gap far outside noise (p < 0.0001). When the two disagree, the market is right three times in five.

Against the spread there is still nothing here worth acting on. 52.7% — 955–857 — which sits three tenths of a point above the 52.4% you need merely to break even on standard juice, with a 95% confidence interval of 50.4–55.0% that comfortably contains break-even. Season by season it runs from 47.0% to 58.6%, which is what noise looks like. Successive feature additions have walked this figure up from 50.4%, and it is the most flattering number in this audit, which is exactly why it deserves the most suspicion: each of those additions was kept partly because numbers like this one moved, so the final value has been selected on, and a selected-on number is biased upward by construction. Filtering to the games where the model disagrees most with the line now flatters it — at gaps of five points or more it goes 54.3% over 492 games — but that is one slice of one sample chosen after seeing it, which is how every spurious betting system ever found has looked at this stage. Treat all of it as a coin flip.

It is overconfident in the middle of its range. When it says a team has a 65–75% chance, that team wins meaningfully less often than that. Only its strongest calls hold up: above 70% confidence it roughly matches the market on those games.

Where it does hold up is the long view. Summed across a season its win probabilities reconstruct team records with a correlation of 0.80 and an average miss of 1.72 wins. Its largest misses are the teams that famously outran their underlying numbers — the 2024 Vikings (predicted 8.9 wins, actual 14) and the 2019 Packers (7.2 vs. 12), both of whom went absurdly well in one-score games. Missing those in that direction is what a working efficiency model is supposed to do.

Method

Team ratings

EPA per play — offense and defense-allowed, split by pass and rush — from nflverse team-week box scores, smoothed with a six-game exponential average. At each season’s start a team is 50% its final rating from last year and 50% the league mean, which is why Week 1 ratings are flatter than 2025 form.

Game model

Gradient-boosted trees on rating differentials, rest-day advantage, the division-game flag, the projected starting quarterback’s own passing and rushing form, whether a team is starting someone other than last week’s quarterback, the share of each side’s recent snaps ruled out on the injury report, and six scheme-identity measures describing how each staff plays — pass-run balance, air yards per attempt, tempo, pass rush, protection and takeaways, each an exponentially-weighted average of that team’s prior games only. Weather is collected and shown but deliberately kept out of the model — it tested as noise. The predicted score splits the market total by the predicted margin.

Player model

Each player’s rolling stat line, adjusted by the opponent’s defensive EPA allowed at the relevant skill, via ridge regression — three terms, which is why every projection can be shown broken into a baseline, his form, the matchup and the venue that add back to the number. Week 1 lines use 2025 season-end form matched to each player’s current team and depth-chart slot.

What each added factor was worth

Walk-forward, 2019–2025, same protocol throughout. Each row is the feature set as a whole, scored on identical folds, so the rows are comparable to each other and to nothing else.

Quarterback identity moved accuracy 61.0% → 61.8%, but McNemar on the paired picks returned p = 0.30 — directionally right, statistically unproven. Weather is the clean negative result: it changed the pick rate by 0.00% and made Brier worse, so it is shown on the site as context and never fed to the model — both teams play in the same wind, so it mostly cancels. Injuries were actively harmful for most of this project’s life. The first version summed every ruled-out player’s snap share, which made five rotational backups look like one lost left tackle and treated a starting corner like a starting guard; it picked winners worse than having no injury features at all (61.2% against 61.8%). Weighting the two biggest absences per side by position took it to 62.2%. That is the largest single correction in this table, and it came from fixing a feature rather than adding one. Coaching scheme, offensive and then defensive, bought calibration and not picks: Brier 0.2323 → 0.2305 → 0.2293 and margin error 10.51 → 10.45 → 10.43, while the pick rate ends at 61.9%, slightly below where the offensive block alone had it (p = 0.62). Both are in because better-calibrated probabilities are worth having on their own. Neither is in because it improves the picks, because neither does. The defensive block was also tested as a player-prop input, including as a predictor of carries and targets, where the weighted error moved by 0.001 — how well a defense plays is already in that model as EPA allowed, and it contains nearly everything that how it plays would add.

One caveat applies to every row above. A single XGBoost fit varies by roughly ±0.4 points of pick accuracy from the random seed alone — wider than most of the effects in this table. Every number here is now the mean of an eight-seed ensemble, which is also what the site runs. Before that change the defensive block was measured on one fit, looked clearly useless, and was published here as such; the ensemble reversed it. Any result on this page resting on a difference smaller than half a point should be read as unproven.
