Minimum confidence
Read this before you put money on anything. “Confident” is not “safe,” and it is not “profitable.” Two facts from the Accuracy tab apply here. First, against the spread this model is a coin flip — 51.3% over 1,812 graded games, against a 52.4% break-even, with a confidence interval that straddles it — which is why there are no spread picks on this page at all. Second, straight-up confidence is well calibrated, but a favorite that wins 79% of the time is priced by the market at roughly −376 on the moneyline; to make money you would have to find a book paying better than that, and books do not usually. What this page tells you is which games the model is sure about. It does not tell you which bets have value. Not betting advice.

The honest verdict

It is a real model, and it is worse than Vegas. Picking winners at 63.3% is decisively better than chance (p < 10−20) and nine points better than blindly taking the home team. But the closing market line picks at 66.6%, and a McNemar test on the paired predictions puts that gap far outside noise (p < 0.0001). When the two disagree, the market is right three times in five.

Against the spread there is nothing here worth acting on, and this page can now prove it. 51.3% — 930–882 — which is a full point below the 52.4% you need merely to break even on standard juice, with a 95% confidence interval of 49.0–53.6%. Season by season it runs from 44.6% to 56.7%, which is what noise looks like.

The number used to read 52.7%, just above break-even, and this page said in this paragraph that it was the most flattering figure in the audit, that successive feature additions had walked it up from 50.4%, and that a number selected on in that way is biased upward by construction. Then a change that improved everything else — picks, calibration and margin error all moved the right way — sent it down through break-even. That is the warning being collected on rather than an argument to go back. Nothing about the spread here has ever been an edge, and the one time it looked like one was the sample flattering itself.

It is overconfident in the middle of its range. When it says a team has a 65–75% chance, that team wins meaningfully less often than that. Only its strongest calls hold up: above 70% confidence it roughly matches the market on those games.

Where it does hold up is the long view. Summed across a season its win probabilities reconstruct team records with a correlation of 0.80 and an average miss of 1.62 wins. Its largest misses are the teams that famously outran their underlying numbers — the 2024 Vikings (predicted 9.3 wins, actual 14) and the 2019 Packers (7.3 vs. 12), both of whom went absurdly well in one-score games. Missing those in that direction is what a working efficiency model is supposed to do.

Method

Team ratings

EPA per play — offense and defense-allowed, split by pass and rush — from nflverse team-week box scores, smoothed with a six-game exponential average. At each season’s start a team is 50% its final rating from last year and 50% the league mean, which is why Week 1 ratings are flatter than 2025 form.

Game model

Gradient-boosted trees on rating differentials, rest-day advantage, the division-game flag, the projected starting quarterback’s own passing and rushing form, whether a team is starting someone other than last week’s quarterback, the share of each side’s recent snaps ruled out on the injury report, and six scheme-identity measures describing how each staff plays — pass-run balance, air yards per attempt, tempo, pass rush, protection and takeaways, each an exponentially-weighted average of that team’s prior games only. Weather is collected and shown but deliberately kept out of the model — it tested as noise. The predicted score splits the market total by the predicted margin.

Player model

Each player’s rolling stat line, adjusted by the opponent’s defensive EPA allowed at the relevant skill, via ridge regression — three terms, which is why every projection can be shown broken into a baseline, his form, the matchup and the venue that add back to the number. Week 1 lines use 2025 season-end form matched to each player’s current team and depth-chart slot.

What each added factor was worth

Walk-forward, 2019–2025, same protocol throughout. Each row is the feature set as a whole, scored on identical folds, so the rows are comparable to each other and to nothing else.

Quarterback identity moved accuracy 61.0% → 61.8%, but McNemar on the paired picks returned p = 0.30 — directionally right, statistically unproven. Weather is the clean negative result: it changed the pick rate by 0.00% and made Brier worse, so it is shown on the site as context and never fed to the model — both teams play in the same wind, so it mostly cancels. Injuries were actively harmful for most of this project’s life. The first version summed every ruled-out player’s snap share, which made five rotational backups look like one lost left tackle and treated a starting corner like a starting guard; it picked winners worse than having no injury features at all (61.2% against 61.8%). Weighting the two biggest absences per side by position took it to 62.3%. That is the largest single correction in this table, and it came from fixing a feature rather than adding one. Coaching scheme, offensive and then defensive, bought calibration and not picks: Brier 0.2322 → 0.2305 → 0.2294 and margin error 10.51 → 10.45 → 10.42, while the pick rate lands at 61.9%, slightly below where the offensive block alone had it (p = 0.17). Both are in because better-calibrated probabilities are worth having on their own. Neither is in because it improves the picks, because neither does. The defensive block was also tested as a player-prop input, including as a predictor of carries and targets, where the weighted error moved by 0.001 — how well a defense plays is already in that model as EPA allowed, and it contains nearly everything that how it plays would add.

The last row is the humbling one. Every factor above describes how a team plays. None of them had ever been checked against the dumbest available alternative: a rating that knows only who beat whom, updated after each game, with no EPA, no quarterback, no injuries and no scheme. That rating picks 63.3% on its own — a hair better than the entire feature set it took this whole project to build, with a better Brier and a better log loss.

Reading that as “ratings beat features” would be wrong, and the check that shows why is worth more than the result. Freeze the same rating at the start of each season, so it knows only prior years, and it drops to 60.2% — worse than the feature model. All of its advantage comes from updating after every game. What it is really measuring is that a rating system tracks in-season form better than exponentially-weighted EPA does, using exactly what EPA per play throws away: the result, including special teams, field position and every close game that went one way rather than the other.

The two disagree on 24% of games and the rating takes those 53–47, so they know different things, and combining them is worth more than any factor added before it: picks 61.9% → 63.3%, Brier 0.2294 → 0.2259, margin error 10.42 → 10.29. It is also the first change that pushed the blended number past the market, 66.5% → 67.1% against the line’s 66.6%. Two things stop that being a victory lap. Against the spread it went the other way, 52.7% → 51.3%, and on the blended picks McNemar returns p = 0.14 — better in five of seven seasons, tied in one, but unproven. And the rating alone, once blended with the market, adds nothing at all (66.6%, the market’s own number): it is largely telling the line what the line already knows. The features are what make the combination beat it.

Play-by-play detail was the obvious next thing to try, and it did nothing. Everything above is built from nflverse’s weekly team totals, which carry EPA but not the shape of how it was earned. Five measures that only exist at the play level were built and tested: success rate, explosive-play rate, early-down EPA, third-down conversion and red-zone efficiency, each for a team’s own offense and for what its defense allowed, smoothed exactly like the EPA ratings. The reasoning was sound — EPA per play is a mean, and a mean cannot tell a team that is steadily fine from one that is mostly bad with three explosions.

All five together made it worse: Brier 0.2259 → 0.2266, log loss 0.6453 → 0.6472, picks flat. Split into smaller blocks nothing survived either. Success and explosive rate moved picks +0.2 points, inside the ±0.4 of seed noise, with Brier and log loss unchanged to four decimals. The situational pair looked best on picks at +0.5 points but made the probabilities worse on both measures (p = 0.37), which is the signature of a feature that is fitting the sample rather than the game. The code is kept but not wired in, because downloading 220 MB of play-by-play every run to move nothing is not free.

One caveat applies to every row above. A single XGBoost fit varies by roughly ±0.4 points of pick accuracy from the random seed alone — wider than most of the effects in this table. Every number here is now the mean of an eight-seed ensemble, which is also what the site runs. Before that change the defensive block was measured on one fit, looked clearly useless, and was published here as such; the ensemble reversed it. Any result on this page resting on a difference smaller than half a point should be read as unproven.
